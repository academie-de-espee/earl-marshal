*A guide for marshals*

Inclusive practices don't happen by accident — they take deliberate structure, attentive marshaling, and a culture that's set intentionally rather than left to default. This guide collects practical steps for making fight practices and events more welcoming to women and gender-minority fighters, organized by the part of practice they apply to.

## Structure the practice

* Build a consistent portion of every practice around drills and training, not just open sparring, and hold it at the same time each week so fighters can plan around it.
* Consider a short beginner-focused session before the main practice, so new fighters get foundational instruction without feeling like they are slowing down the group.
* If beginner focused sessions can't be done beforehand, consider a specified area set aside for less experienced fighters that they can 'self graduate' from at their pace.

## Teach and pair deliberately

* During drills, pair fighters strategically: put newer fighters with more experienced ones, or rotate pairs, rather than letting new fighters only drill with each other.
* In larger drills, make sure attention — yours and the experienced fighters' — goes to the least experienced people in the group, not just the advanced ones.
* Watch for fighters who may be new, shy, or otherwise easy to overlook, and offer to match them with a partner, both in structured drills and when the field opens for pick-ups afterward. Being overlooked for such activities is a commonly reported experience among women and gender-minority fighters.
* Keep body-type differences in mind when teaching technique. What works well for you may not work as well for someone shorter, taller, younger, older, or with different joints, physical abilities, muscle strength, or wrist width.
* Be mindful of how you offer advice. Instructors tend to interrupt female fighters with unsolicited feedback more than they would male fighters. Let each fighter set the level and timing of instruction, and respect it when they haven't asked for more.
* Be respectful of the person who indicates that they do not want the feedback right now. This might sound like "My knight/teacher wants me to work on something else right now." Or some other deflection. Don't be offended that your words are not sacred.

## Provide the right gear

* Getting gear is one of the biggest barriers to starting a martial sport. Most loaner armor and loaner swords are sized and weighted for a "typical" SCA fighter, which usually means they don't fit smaller fighters well or aren't light enough for them to wield comfortably. Stock loaner gear, including lighter swords, sized for smaller fighters.
* Periodically check your loaner inventory against who's actually showing up, and ask newer fighters directly whether the gear fits — sizing gaps are easy to miss if no one mentions them.

## Set the tone on conduct

* Watch for inappropriate behavior at practice and make clear, consistently, that it isn't acceptable.
* Share a basic code of conduct so expectations are clear before an issue comes up, not only after.
* Be especially watchful for fighters who tend to ignore shots from women and gender-minority fighters, and be ready to intervene. This is a case where active marshaling on the field, and a private conversation off it, are both appropriate.
* Where you can, put women and gender-minority fighters in visible roles — as marshals, trainers, or in exhibition bouts — so newer fighters can see people like themselves succeeding.

## Assign clear roles and stick to a plan

* Identify your marshals and publicize planned activities in advance, and stick to that schedule. It's fine to adjust the activities themselves based on turnout, but a reliable timetable matters most for fighters juggling family schedules.
* Consider designating an authorized trainer for the practice, distinct from the safety marshal, who has training expertise and has taken at least one course specifically on coaching women and gender-minority fighters.
* Consider designating an ombudsperson so new fighters know who to raise concerns with.
* Periodically ask attendees, informally or anonymously, how the practice feels to them. Climate problems are often invisible to organizers until someone quietly stops showing up.

## Consideration in Tourneys

* Be strategic about when you call breaks, particularly in tournaments. Fighters shouldn't overheat or fight past the point of good control, but breaks also tend to favor sprint- or power-oriented athletes over endurance-oriented ones — factor that trade-off into the timing of breaks, not just physical safety. Advertising an endurance event and then switching it into an event with many breaks removes any endurance advantage a person may have entered for.

## Selecting or rebalancing teams

* Be deliberate when creating and/or re-balancing teams; avoid methods that provide an opportunity for, or give the impression of, bias. As examples, (1) avoid having two team captains who then select all of their team members (where "in group" members get chosen early, and fighters from underrepresented groups get left until the end); (2) avoid patterns that end up moving women or fighters from underrepresented groups off the less successful team as your main lever for re-balancing — rank fighters by skill and split by odds/evens, or another method disconnected from identity.

## Make the logistics work for families

* Hold practice at a public location, not a private home. Many women will be uncomfortable attending a practice at an unfamiliar man's home, especially without knowing how many other women will be there
* Coordinate martial activities with youth activities where possible, so fighters with young children can participate knowing their kids are supervised and having a good time. And if an event has multiple sequential activities, stick to planned start times, so fighters who arranged for child care for part of the day don't find themselves needing to miss the very activities they had most wanted to participate in.
* Consider basic accessibility — parking, seating, restroom access — for fighters who are pregnant, have disabilities, or otherwise need accommodation.

## Terminology matters

* Use gender-neutral terms ("five-fighter teams" rather than "five-man teams"; "you're dead from behind" rather than "my lord/lady, you're dead").
* Avoid gendered phrases ("cry like a girl," "fight like a girl," "take it like a man," "man up").
